Coastal tourism in Australia

Where visitors go, when they travel, and how much coastal economies depend on them.

Part 01

Where the visitors go

Australia has 76 tourism regions. Forty-one of them meet the open coast, and that is where almost all of the industry sits.

When they travel

The coast does not have one season. It has two, running in opposite directions, and a national rhythm that survived a two-year interruption.

Conclusion

A shoreline economy

Three measures, taken together, describe an industry that lives almost entirely within sight of the water.

Australian tourism is coastal tourism. Most of its visitors are Australians taking short trips to a nearby stretch of coast, arriving on seasonal calendars that run in opposite directions in the tropics and the south.

Concentration

87%

of Australia's 696,000 direct tourism jobs sit in coastal regions, and the five largest tourism employers in the country are all on the coast.

Proximity

82%

of measured overnight spending comes from domestic travellers, most of them moving within their own state rather than across the country.

Divergence

2×

is roughly the swing between a region's busiest and quietest quarter — and the tropics and the south reach those extremes in opposite months.

What complicates it

Scale and momentum are not the same thing. Although coastal regions hold almost nine in ten tourism jobs, the median coastal region grew more slowly than the median inland one over the latest year, 1.8% against 2.6%.

Western Australia's coast lost ground while Queensland's gained, so the headline concentration conceals sharply different local trajectories. A region being coastal explains where the industry is, not whether it is growing.

What to treat with caution

Three methodology breaks sit underneath this data: the move from the National Visitor Survey to Domestic Tourism Statistics in 2025, a new sub-national satellite account model in 2024–25 that was only back-cast one year at region level, and a downward revision of tourism employment for 2018–19 from 700,900 to 632,000.

The coastal and inland split is also my own classification rather than an official category, reporting periods differ between states, and charts 3, 4, 5 and 6 contain placeholder values where no source publishes a regional figure.
